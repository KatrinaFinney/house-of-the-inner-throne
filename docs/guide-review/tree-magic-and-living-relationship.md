## Choosing a Tree

Start with a tree you can return to. Relationship grows through attention.

### A Hoodoo Foundation

This guide begins with African American Hoodoo: living rootwork traditions in which particular roots, barks, and other natural materials have specific spiritual uses. Tree relationship also appears in contemporary practitioner accounts. Families and workers differ; there is no single tree rite shared by everyone.

We distinguish documented correspondences from original Shrine practices. The invitations below to speak, listen, and bring a concern are spiritual exercises, not promises that a tree will solve a problem. Broader African diasporic tree traditions deserve their own names and contexts.

### Choose with Both Feeling and Knowledge

- Access: choose a garden, yard, or public-space tree you may legally visit. Obtain permission on private land.
- Recognition: notice which tree draws your attention, then learn its species. A familiar tree is enough.
- Purpose: consider protection, steadiness, prosperity, remembrance, or simply companionship.
- Condition: choose a safe place to sit or stand. Avoid storm-damaged trees, hanging limbs, and visits during thunderstorms.
- Continuity: a younger garden tree can become a long-term companion; an older tree can invite reflection on endurance. Size does not establish spiritual rank.

### Your First Visit

Introduce yourself in ordinary words: your name, why you came, and your wish to return respectfully. Ask permission within your spiritual practice before touching or gathering. Keep landowner permission and tree health in mind as separate responsibilities. You do not need to take anything.

## Trees and Their Uses

Learn what is documented, and leave room for the tree you actually know.

### Documented Rootwork Uses - Sources 1-4

- White oak | Use: cleansing, uncrossing, spiritual protection from harmful conditions. Source 1 documents bark work; it does not mean living bark should be stripped.
- Pine | Use: spiritual cleansing, steady money. Source 2 records these associations. Identify the species before collecting any needles.
- Sassafras | Use: money luck, business prosperity, retaining money. Source 3 documents root curios; this guide does not teach ingestion or root harvesting.
- Bayberry | Use: money drawing, prosperity work. Source 4 concerns bayberry root, not culinary bay leaves; the two are different plants.

### Garden Trees - Shrine Interpretations

- Pecan or another nut tree | Explore abundance, preparation, and sharing through its actual harvest. This is a Shrine reading, not an asserted historical pecan correspondence.
- Fig or another fruit tree | Explore nourishment, patient cultivation, and the relationship between care and fruitfulness.
- An established shade tree | Explore shelter, endurance, and the steadiness you seek in a difficult season.
- A young tree you tend | Explore beginnings, commitment, and growth over time. The relationship need not wait for an ancient tree.

### A Tree Is More Than an Ingredient

The same species can grow in very different conditions. Notice the tree's place, season, surrounding life, and history. Do not assign an age without evidence or assume all trees of a species have the same spiritual character.

## Speaking and Listening

Bring your words. Allow quiet to be part of the exchange.

### A First Conversation

1. Stand or sit comfortably near your chosen tree. Introduce yourself and observe its trunk, canopy, and surroundings.
2. Name one concern without performing or exaggerating. Say what kind of support you seek: courage, protection, clarity, or endurance.
3. Ask one simple question, such as: 'What would steadiness look like in this situation?'
4. Rest quietly for several minutes. Notice feelings, memories, and ideas without forcing an answer.
5. Write down your impressions as impressions. Wind, leaf movement, and animal activity are not automatic proof of a message.
6. Thank the tree. Choose one practical action before you leave.

> May I approach with respect, listen with patience, and carry wisdom into my choices.

### When Something Feels Too Large

A mature tree can become a spiritual place to bring a substantial burden. Our Shrine practice is to name what you carry, ask for strength to meet it, and imagine being supported by deep roots and a wide canopy. This is an original devotional exercise; it is not presented as a universal Hoodoo rite.

Write two lists afterward: what you can act on and what requires help from other people. Return to the tree for reflection while continuing the conversations, decisions, or professional support the situation requires.

## Protection, Power, and Abundance

Choose one small practice and return with care.

### Three Shrine Practices

- Protection: speak a household blessing beside a tree you know. Name one boundary you will uphold. Record the prayer at home; leave the tree free of attached objects.
- Power: bring a difficult decision. Rest a hand lightly on the trunk if appropriate, or sit nearby. Name the strength you need and one action you will take.
- Abundance: visit a productive garden tree. Reflect on tending, receiving, and sharing. Pair your prayer with appropriate care or a realistic plan for your resources.

### A Garden Relationship

Mark the tree in your garden journal. Learn its needs, observe buds and fruit, and return through the seasons. Offer care appropriate to its species and soil conditions; more water is not always helpful. Pruning should follow sound horticultural guidance, not a ritual impulse.

A memorial planting can honor someone or a former tree companion. A Lucky Mojo practitioner discussion suggests planting the same species to renew a personal tree connection (Source 5). That is contemporary advice, not a rule every reader must follow.

## Return with Reciprocity

The relationship includes the tree's continued life.

### Leave the Tree Well

- Do not strip bark, dig roots, drive nails, carve names, tie constricting cords, or bury glass at the roots.
- Prefer observation, drawings, photographs, or permitted fallen material. Fallen material can still be habitat; take little or nothing.
- Keep salt, oils, wax, glitter, sweeteners, and alcohol out of the soil. Remove litter without disturbing wildlife.
- Record the date, species, request, impression, and action. Do not require a sign before making an ordinary decision.
